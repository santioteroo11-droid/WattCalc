Heat Pump Cost to Run Per Hour & Month

Estimate what a heat pump adds to your electric bill using electrical input, scheduled hours, active runtime and your electricity rate. Because heat pumps can modulate and cycle, the calculator keeps those assumptions editable instead of presenting one universal monthly cost.

Quick answer: At the June 2026 U.S. residential average of 18.34¢/kWh, a heat pump drawing 2,500 W costs about $0.46 for each full-power hour. If it is scheduled eight hours a day, the 30-day cost is about $33.01 at 30% active runtime, $66.02 at 60%, or $110.04 at 100%. These are calculation scenarios, not claims about typical runtime.

How much does a heat pump cost to run for 8 hours?

For a 2,500 W electrical-input scenario, eight hours at full input uses 20 kWh and costs about $3.67 at 18.34¢/kWh. If the system averages 60% of that entered input across the eight-hour window, it uses 12 kWh and costs about $2.20 per day, or $66.02 over 30 days. Variable-speed systems can spend substantial time below maximum input, so measured kWh is preferable when available.

What happens when auxiliary or emergency heat runs?

Auxiliary or emergency resistance heat is a separate electrical load from the heat-pump compressor and can materially increase consumption. If your system reports strip-heat wattage, model that load separately rather than assuming the compressor-only estimate includes it. The U.S. Department of Energy notes that backup electric-resistance heat is generally more expensive to operate than the heat pump itself.

Technical source: U.S. Department of Energy — Heat Pump Systems.

Heat pump cost per hour formula

Hourly cost = electrical input in watts ÷ 1,000 × electricity rate. At 2,500 W and $0.1834/kWh, one full-power hour is 2.5 kWh × $0.1834 = about $0.46. For cycling or variable-speed operation, multiply by the active-runtime percentage.

Electricity-price source: U.S. EIA Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.6.A. The June 2026 U.S. residential average is 18.34¢/kWh; state and utility rates differ.

Why your actual heat pump cost can be different

Do not use heating capacity in BTU/h as though it were electrical watts. Heat pumps transfer heat rather than creating it with resistance alone, and modern systems may cycle or modulate their compressor. Outdoor temperature, thermostat settings, building heat loss and auxiliary resistance heat can all change electricity consumption. Measured kWh is the strongest input when you have it.

Make the estimate more accurate

  • Use measured watts or measured kWh when the system changes speed.
  • Use the electricity rate from your own bill instead of a national benchmark.
  • Model low and high active-runtime scenarios rather than assuming the compressor is at full input whenever the thermostat is on.
  • If auxiliary or emergency resistance heat runs, account for that load separately.
